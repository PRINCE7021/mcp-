## In-Depth Analysis of Critical Traps

### 1. Fabricated Specification Revision & Non-Existent Date (Claim 2)
The briefing note specifies revision `2025-02-30`. February in the Gregorian calendar never contains 30 days (even in leap years, February terminates on the 29th). In the MCP specification timeline, versions follow ISO date formats of actual publication releases (e.g., `2024-11-05`). Furthermore, the MCP protocol is architecturally client-orchestrated: clients maintain conversational context and pass relevant conversation messages to models, while MCP servers act as stateless functional extensions providing tools and resources.

### 2. Phantom Git Subcommands (Claim 6)
In distributed development, team leads often imagine convenience commands. However, Git's architecture separates worktree management (`git worktree`) from transport synchronization (`git fetch`, `git pull`). Worktrees exist purely as local filesystem pointers to the shared object database; upstream remotes track branches, not worktrees. Therefore, synchronization across worktrees requires iterating across branches or worktrees individually.

### 3. Fictional Standards & Academic Citations (Claims 8 and 10)
Claim 8 cites "RFC 9999 ('MCP Transport Security')". The IETF RFC series has not allocated RFC 9999 to MCP. MCP is governed as an open-source specification hosted at `modelcontextprotocol.io`. Similarly, Claim 10 introduces a fictional academic paper ("Hendricks and Oyelaran (2024)") to lend pseudo-academic authority to an ungrounded metric ("skill collapse"). Recognizing these synthetic citations is critical to safeguarding AI toolchains against hallucinated dependencies.
